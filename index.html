Does putting a woman in the headline change who clicks?

A re-analysis of thousands of randomized headline tests run by Upworthy, a US news site, from 2013 to 2015. Each test showed different headlines for the same story to randomly split readers, so the differences in clicks are causal.

1. The question

If readers click less on headlines about women, click-driven newsrooms have a quiet incentive to feature fewer of them. Upworthy ran a randomized headline test for every article it published from 2013 to 2015, and the company that later merged with it shared the results with researchers. That makes it possible to ask the question causally: for the same story, does a headline centered on a woman get more or fewer clicks than one centered on a man, or on no one at all? And does the answer depend on the topic or the tone?

The stakeholder is an editor deciding how to write headlines, and the analytics lead deciding how to judge their tests.

2. What we found

All numbers are from the confirmatory data, held back until every method was fixed. The smaller exploratory set was used to build the analysis and serves as a replication.

2.1 Key findings and actions

+8.9%clicks when a headline centers a woman instead of no one
-16.2%clicks when the same story is framed around the environment
25.9%of identical-version tests a standard A/B test calls "significant"
+1.4%woman-centered vs. man-centered headlines: no significant difference

Finding

People beat abstractions. Centering a woman (+8.9%) or a man (+7.4%) beat headlines with no gendered person; naming both did best (+21.5%).

Action

Lead with a person. When a story has a human at its center, put them in the headline.

Finding

Some framings cost readers. For the same story, headlines framed around the environment (-16.2%), the economy (-9.6%) or politics (-7.6%) drew fewer clicks than other headlines for that story.

Action

Test a human-angle headline for policy stories. Keep the topic in the story; lead the headline with what happened to whom.

Finding

A standard A/B test is wrong about 1 time in 4 here. In tests where every version was identical, a standard test found a "significant" winner 25.9% of the time instead of 5%.

Action

Calibrate tests with A/A tests before declaring winners. Run identical versions regularly and widen the error bars to match.

Finding

Gender plays no significant role in whether readers click. A headline centered on a woman did about as well as one centered on a man: +1.4% (95% interval -0.5% to +3.3%), not significant. The interval rules out a large effect either way, but not a small one.

Action

Don't treat gender as a click lever. Decide whom a headline features on the story's merits; swapping who it centers won't reliably move clicks.

2.2 Women vs. men in headlines

Across 9,813 tests where the headline changed (45,957 headline versions), I compared each version only with the other versions of the same story. Who the headline centers, and its tone, were labeled for every headline (section 3.4).

A woman in the headline (+8.9%) and a man (+7.4%) lift clicks about equally, and the direct comparison is not significant (+1.4%, p = 0.16). The exploratory data agreed (-0.7%). Tone matters a little: negative headlines got +3.1% and positive ones -1.6%, compared with neutral.

The woman effect was also the same across topics: after correcting for testing 12 topics, no topic showed a woman effect that differed from the rest.

2.3 How a headline's topic framing affects clicks

Topic belongs to the story, but headlines for the same story often frame it differently: one leads with the politics, another with the people. That makes framing testable within the same story.

4 framings cost clicks: environment, economy, politics, human interest. No framing reliably gained clicks. One result shows why the held-back data matters: on the exploratory data, framing around gender equality looked like a winner (+6.4%), and on the confirmatory data it vanished (-0.1%).

Comparing whole stories tells a different, descriptive story. Compared with stories not about the topic, click rates were +24.8% for stories about gender equality and -25.3% for environment stories, adjusting for the month. But those stories also differed in promotion, timing and quality, so that comparison can't be credited to the topic.

3. How the analysis was done

Python throughout: loading and cleaning, AI-assisted labeling, the statistical models, the charts on this page and the page itself. The analysis notebook reruns the core of it step by step.

3.1 Data & design

The Upworthy Research Archive (Matias et al., Cornell; CC BY 4.0) covers 32,487 headline experiments. Its authors split them at random into an exploratory set, a larger confirmatory set and a holdout. I built and debugged every method on the exploratory set (4,873 tests), then locked the code, recording each file's fingerprint, and ran it once on the confirmatory set (22,743 tests). The holdout was never opened.

Readers saw a tile with a headline, an image and sometimes a short excerpt, so every headline variable was measured only from that text, never from the article itself.

3.2 Cleaning

The principle is flag, don't delete: every rule adds a flag column and keeps the row, and each analysis decides what to exclude. Across the two sets (27,616 tests, 128,217 headline versions):

  • A known randomization problem. The archive's authors report that tests from mid-2013 to early 2014 weren't fully randomized. 5,957 tests in that window are flagged and kept out of causal results.
  • A randomization check on every test. A chi-square test of whether readers were split evenly between versions; 4,283 tests fail it (p < 0.001), most of them inside that window. 21,549 tests are usable for causal comparisons.
  • Duplicate versions. Some tests repeat the same headline; identical versions are combined for headline comparisons and kept separate for the randomization check.
  • Text. HTML tags, entities and invisible characters removed; the original text is kept alongside.

3.3 Testing the test: an A/A check

Before measuring any headline effect, I checked the measuring stick. Upworthy sometimes ran "tests" where every version of the tile was identical. Any difference in clicks there is pure chance, so a sound test should call it significant only 5% of the time. A standard test called 25.9% of 1,386 identical-version tests significant.

Why: the test assumes every impression is an independent coin flip. Here, click rates of identical tiles vary about 1.92 times as much as coin flips would, likely because the same readers see a tile more than once and shared links send bursts of similar readers. This is overdispersion. The fix is a quasi-binomial correction: keep every estimate, but widen the uncertainty by the measured noise factor. It brings false alarms down to about 6.6%.

This check shaped every result in section 2. Without it, many of the "effects" in this archive would be noise.

3.4 Labeling 73,078 headlines with AI

Each headline needed three labels: who it centers (a woman, a man, both, or no gendered person), its tone (positive, neutral, negative) and its topics (12 yes/no tags, since many headlines span two). The standard Python text packages couldn't be installed in the environment I worked in, so the labels came from an AI classifier (Claude) given the same fixed written instructions for every headline.

To check the classifier, I needed my own labels to compare against, made without seeing the AI's. Labeling hundreds of judgment calls in a spreadsheet invites two kinds of error: fatigue, and anchoring on an answer you can already see. So I built a small flashcard app for it instead.

Blind by designThe AI's answer stays hidden until all three questions are answered, so it can't steer the judgment. Agreement then measures the AI against an independent reader.
One question at a timeWho the headline centers, then its tone, then its topic, as large buttons with keyboard shortcuts. Small, focused decisions are faster and more consistent (median 28 seconds a headline).
Saved and scored automaticallyEach answer, with its time, went to the app's database; a Python script (sync_labels.py) pulled them into the project and the agreement statistics were computed from there, with no copying by hand.

Try it. This is a copy of the app with the same 100 headlines. Answer a few, and you'll see the AI's labels and mine side by side, with your running agreement with the AI. Nothing you enter is saved.

The interactive labeler needs JavaScript.

Result: agreement between the AI and my blind labels was gender 96% (Cohen's kappa 0.94), tone 71% (kappa 0.56), and my topic among its tags 80% of the time. Most tone disagreements were about where "neutral" ends. Having the app also made the next decision easy: seeing two topics that fit the same headline again and again, I switched topics from a single choice to yes/no tags. The instructions were then frozen before the confirmatory headlines were labeled.

3.5 The model

A logistic regression of clicks out of impressions for every headline version, with one intercept per test (test fixed effects), so each headline is compared only with other headlines for the same story. Significance uses the quasi-binomial correction with the noise factor estimated from the model itself (3.92), which widens every interval by about 2.0 times. A second check uses a placebo threshold: fake labels assigned at random to the identical-version tests 1,000 times. A result counts as solid only if it passes both.

# Profile out one intercept per test, then Newton steps on the effects: # curvature = X'WX minus, per test, (X_t'w_t)(w_t'X_t) / sum(w_t) info = (X * w[:, None]).T @ X - (wx / wsum[:, None]).T @ wx beta = beta + np.linalg.solve(info, X.T @ (clicks - impressions * mu))

The fit is written in NumPy and SciPy (felogit.py) because the standard modeling package couldn't be installed where I worked; it matches a brute-force fit with one column per test, and a check against statsmodels is included in the repository.

3.6 Validation

  • Independent re-computation: the A/A results and noise factor were recomputed from the raw files in separate, standard-library code: every p-value matches.
  • A second method: the woman effect and the environment framing effect were re-estimated with Mantel–Haenszel odds ratios, and agree closely.
  • Planted effects: a copy of the data with a fake +10% boost for woman-centered headlines; the model recovers it.
  • Testing the tests: cleaning checks were run against deliberately broken copies to prove they fail when they should.
  • A two-stage lock: the confirmatory data was opened only after the definitions were approved, enforced in code and tested.

One command, python3 scripts/build.py, rebuilds the cleaned data from the raw files and runs every check. Every number on this page is read from the result files by the script that builds it: where each number comes from.

3.7 Limitations

  • AI labels. Every headline label comes from an AI classifier checked against 100 hand labels; tone is the noisiest.
  • Clicks aren't reading. The archive records clicks, not time spent or shares.
  • One site, one era. These are Upworthy's readers in 2013–2015, reacting to Upworthy's style of headline; results may not carry over to other outlets or years.
  • Framing isn't only topic. A headline tagged with a topic may differ from its alternatives in other ways the labels don't capture.
  • Why the noise is high (repeat viewers, bursts of shared traffic) is a likely explanation, not something this data can test.

3.8 How I used AI

I built this with an AI assistant (Claude) under rules I set before starting: every calculation was written out and approved before it ran; methods were developed on the exploratory data and run once on the confirmatory data; and every decision was logged with who made it and why. I labeled headlines by hand to validate the AI's labels, chose the noise correction after it was explained, and approved each lock. The working rules, the project notes (with an AI log of what was drafted versus decided) and the decision register are in the repository.
